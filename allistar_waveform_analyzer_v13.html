ⓘ What is Q Factor and how do I read it? ▾ expand

Q Factor (Quality Factor) measures how sharp and selective a resonance is — how precisely a system locks onto one specific frequency and resists energy loss to adjacent frequencies.

The formula is simple: Q = f₀ / Δf — the resonant frequency divided by the bandwidth at the −3dB point (where peak amplitude drops to 70.7% of its maximum).

Analogy · Think of a tuning fork versus a drum. A tuning fork rings at one precise frequency for a long time — very high Q. A drum produces a broad thud across many frequencies and decays quickly — very low Q. High Q means the system stores energy efficiently and releases it at a precise, stable frequency. Low Q means energy dissipates quickly across a wide band.

How to read Q values:

Q < 1
Overdamped — very broad resonance, little energy storage
Q 1–10
Moderate — typical passive components, visible peak
Q 10–100
High — good resonators, LC circuits, quartz crystals
Q > 1000
Very high — optical cavities, atomic clocks, precision resonators

Why it matters for resonance research: A higher Q means your prototype is storing more energy per cycle relative to what it loses. A Q that increases as you adjust geometry or coupling is a direct measurement of your device becoming a more efficient resonator. Tracking Q across experiments reveals whether your design changes are moving toward or away from true resonance.

ⓘ What is Fractal PSD and how do I read it? ▾ expand

PSD (Power Spectral Density) shows how the power of your signal is distributed across frequencies — not as sharp peaks, but as a continuous density curve. It answers the question: does this signal have more energy at low frequencies, or is it spread evenly?

Analogy · Imagine measuring rainfall across a mountain range. Some areas get heavy rain (high power), others get little (low power). The PSD is the rainfall map of your signal — showing where the energy lives across the frequency landscape.

The spectral slope B tells you the shape of the PSD curve on a log-log chart — how steeply power drops as frequency increases:

B = 0
White noise — flat, random, no structure
B ≈ −1
Pink noise — natural systems, 1/f behaviour
B ≈ −2
Brown noise — random walk, integration
B < −2
Strong low-frequency dominance

Fractal Dimension D is calculated from B as: D = (5 + B) / 2. It describes the self-similarity and complexity of your signal across scales.

  • D ≈ 1.5 — white noise. Completely random, no fractal structure.
  • D ≈ 1.0 — pink noise. Found in heartbeats, music, natural systems. Scale-invariant.
  • D between 1.0–1.5 — your signal has fractal character. It repeats its pattern at multiple scales simultaneously.
Why it matters for resonance research · A signal with fractal PSD structure (D near 1.0) suggests the system is operating near a self-organised critical point — the same regime found in living systems, neural networks, and natural oscillators. If your prototype produces a signal with fractal character, it is behaving like a natural resonator rather than a forced oscillator. This is a significant finding.

R² Fit Quality tells you how reliably the slope B was measured. Above 0.9 is excellent — the power law fits cleanly. Below 0.7 means the slope is not consistent across the selected range and the result should be interpreted with caution.

ⓘ What is THD+N and how do I read it? ▾ expand

THD+N (Total Harmonic Distortion + Noise) measures how much of your signal's total energy is not in the fundamental frequency — it captures all harmonics plus background noise as a single percentage or dB value.

Analogy · Imagine a musician playing a single pure note. A perfect instrument produces only that note — 0% distortion. A real instrument also produces overtones and some background hiss. THD+N measures how much of the total sound is not the intended note. Lower is purer.

How to read the values:

  • THD+N % — percentage of total signal power that is harmonic distortion + noise. Lower = purer signal. Below 1% is excellent for most applications.
  • THD+N dBc — same measurement in decibels relative to the fundamental. More negative = purer. −40 dBc means harmonics are 100× weaker than the fundamental.
  • SFDR (Spurious-Free Dynamic Range) — gap in dB between your fundamental and the strongest unwanted peak. Larger is better. It tells you how much headroom you have before interference becomes a problem.
  • Tone Character (Even/Odd ratio) — even harmonics (2nd, 4th) give a warm, musical sound. Odd harmonics (3rd, 5th) give a harsher, more distorted character. This ratio tells you the harmonic personality of your signal.
THD < 1%
Excellent purity
THD 1–5%
Acceptable
THD > 10%
Heavy distortion
SFDR > 60 dBc
Very clean

In resonance research, unexpectedly low THD at a specific geometry or frequency is a meaningful signal — it suggests the system has found a natural, efficient mode. High SFDR means your fundamental dominates cleanly with minimal interference.

Quick Start — The 5 Rules You Must Know

Rule 1 · Know your target frequency first.
Before setting up your oscilloscope, ask: what frequency am I expecting to see? Write it down. Everything else flows from this number.

Sample Rate = at least 5× to 10× your target frequency

Rule 2 · Set sample rate correctly.

Imagine filming a spinning wheel with a camera. If the camera takes only 2 photos per second and the wheel spins 3 times per second, the footage makes it look like the wheel spins backwards. That optical illusion — caused by sampling too slowly — is called aliasing. It creates fake frequency peaks in your spectrum that are not real.

Resolution (Hz/bin) = 1 ÷ Recording Time (seconds)

Rule 3 · Record long enough. The longer you record, the finer your frequency resolution — the closer two peaks can be and still appear as separate spikes.

5 seconds0.2 Hz/bin

Rule 4 · Enter sample rate exactly. When you load your CSV, enter the sample rate exactly as shown on your oscilloscope. If this number is wrong, every peak appears at the wrong frequency.

FFT Size ↑ = Resolution ↑ — always use the largest available

Rule 5 · Use the largest FFT size.

FFT size is like the number of pixels in a photograph. A 100-pixel photo shows a blurry shape. A 10-million-pixel photo shows every sharp detail. More samples = sharper frequency image.

Reading Your Spectrum

The horizontal axis = frequency in Hz. The vertical axis = amplitude (signal strength in dB).

  • Tallest peak = your fundamental frequency — the main frequency your device operates at.
  • Peaks at 2×, 3×, 4× the fundamental = harmonics. These are natural and expected in every real electrical signal — not errors.
  • Noise floor = the flat background carpet. Real peaks should stand clearly above it.
  • Narrow sharp spike above floor = real signal. Wide fuzzy hump near the floor = noise.
A guitar string produces not just one note — it also vibrates at twice, three times, four times the frequency simultaneously. All those vibrations together make the rich tone you hear. Electrical signals behave exactly the same way. Harmonics are the overtones of your fundamental.

Waveform View
Visualizing your raw signal over time
▾

The waveform is the most fundamental view of your signal — it shows voltage on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. Every other measurement in this tool is derived from this raw data.

Vpp — Peak to Peak Voltage
The total swing from the lowest to the highest point of your signal. A sine wave of ±1.5V has a Vpp of 3.0V. This tells you the full energy range of your field.
Vrms — Root Mean Square
The effective voltage — the DC equivalent that would deliver the same power. For a pure sine wave, Vrms = Vpp ÷ 2.83. This is what your components actually experience.
Zoom & Scroll
Zoom in to inspect individual cycles. Use scroll to move through the capture. At 1× you see the full capture; at 20× you see 1/20th of the signal — useful for checking waveform shape and detecting glitches.
Fundamental Frequency
The dominant repeating frequency in your signal, automatically detected via FFT. For a clean oscillator this should match your target frequency exactly. Deviation indicates drift or instability.
RESEARCH TIP
For the Space-Time Antenna, capture at least 10 full cycles at your resonant frequency. A clean sine at exactly 4.5 MHz with stable Vpp confirms healthy resonance. Irregular Vpp over time indicates the spark gap is inconsistent.
FFT Spectrum Analysis
Decomposing your signal into its frequency components
▾

FFT stands for Fast Fourier Transform. It takes your raw time-domain signal and mathematically decomposes it into all the individual frequencies it contains. Where the waveform shows you when things happen, the FFT shows you what frequencies are present.

Each spike on the FFT chart represents a frequency component. The height of the spike represents its strength. A clean oscillator produces one sharp tall spike. A complex or noisy signal produces many spikes.

FFT Size
Larger FFT size = better frequency resolution but slower. 512 is fast and rough. 4096 reveals closely spaced frequencies but needs more samples. Start with 1024 for most measurements.
Window Function
Hann — best for general use, reduces spectral leakage. Blackman — best for finding weak signals next to strong ones. Rectangular — use only for perfectly periodic signals.
Linear vs dB Scale
Linear shows absolute magnitude — the dominant peak towers over everything. dB (decibel) scale compresses the range so you can see weak harmonics that would otherwise be invisible at the bottom of the chart.
Detected Peaks
The tool automatically finds the 5 strongest frequency peaks and labels them with gold dots. The brightest dot is the dominant frequency. Values in brackets are the relative magnitude of each peak.
RESEARCH TIP
Switch to dB scale and use Blackman window when looking for harmonic overtones in your Phi Ratio Cones or Flash Matrix. Weak harmonics at 2× and 3× the fundamental are often only visible in dB mode — they confirm the structure is resonating as a system, not just at the drive frequency.
Noise Floor & Baseline Subtraction
Understanding and eliminating background interference
▾

The noise floor is the minimum level of background electrical noise present in your measurement system — even when no signal is intentionally being sent. It is the fundamental limit of what you can measure. Any signal weaker than your noise floor is invisible.

Your signal ████████████ ← what you want
────────────────────────────────
Noise floor ░░░░░░░░░░░░ ← background noise
────────────────────────────────
True zero ____________ ← does not exist

Noise comes from many sources: thermal noise in resistors and wiring, mains interference at 50/60Hz from your power supply, electromagnetic pickup from nearby devices, and the oscilloscope's own internal circuitry.

How to Use Baseline Subtraction
1. With your prototype powered off, capture a CSV from your oscilloscope
2. Load it as Channel 1 and click Capture Noise Baseline in the FFT tab
3. Now power your prototype and capture the measurement signal
4. Load the new signal and enable Noise Subtraction overlay
5. The FFT now shows only what your prototype is adding above the background
RESEARCH TIP
A noise floor above 50mV usually means a grounding problem. For serious RF work, your noise floor should be under 5mV. If you see a large 50Hz spike in your noise baseline, your oscilloscope probe is picking up mains interference — twist your probe cable or move away from power supplies.
Phase Analysis — Channel 2
Understanding the relationship between two measurement points
▾

Phase is the timing relationship between two signals. When two signals have the same frequency, phase tells you whether they are in step with each other or offset in time. It is measured in degrees — a full cycle is 360°.

0° — In Phase
Both signals rise and fall together. The two measurement points are electrically coupled and moving as one. Common in series circuits or tightly coupled coils.
90° — Capacitive Coupling
One signal leads the other by a quarter cycle. This is the signature of a capacitor — voltage lags current by 90°. In field structures this indicates electrostatic coupling dominates.
180° — Anti-Phase
Signals are exact opposites — when one peaks the other troughs. This is the signature of a transformer or opposing field. In standing wave structures, nodes and antinodes are 180° apart.
90° — Inductive Coupling
A 90° shift where current leads voltage — the signature of an inductor. Magnetic field coupling between coils produces this. The sign (leading vs lagging) tells you inductive vs capacitive.
RESEARCH TIP
For the Space-Time Antenna, probe Ch1 at the drive input and Ch2 at the outer copper sphere. If you see 90° phase shift, the sphere is acting as a capacitive element — storing field energy. If you see 180°, the geometry is producing a standing wave condition — this is the more interesting result from an S/T Field perspective.
Schumann Resonances
The Earth's electromagnetic heartbeat
▾

Schumann resonances are standing electromagnetic waves that exist in the cavity between the Earth's surface and the ionosphere. Lightning strikes around the world continuously excite these frequencies. They were first predicted by physicist Winfried Schumann in 1952 and confirmed by measurement in 1960.

The fundamental frequency is 7.83 Hz — determined by the circumference of the Earth divided by the speed of light. Harmonics follow at roughly 14.3, 20.8, 26.4, 32.4 and 39 Hz.

Why These Frequencies Matter
The human brain's alpha wave state (relaxed focus) operates at 8–12 Hz — overlapping with the Schumann fundamental. The theta state (deep meditation) operates at 4–8 Hz. This alignment is why many researchers believe Schumann resonances play a role in biological regulation.

In S/T Field terms, 7.83 Hz represents a natural resonant condition of the Earth-ionosphere system — a geometric standing wave defined by planetary scale. Devices that entrain to or amplify this frequency interact with the largest natural electromagnetic cavity on Earth.
RESEARCH TIP
Enable the Schumann overlay in the FFT tab. Green dashed lines appear at each resonance frequency. If any of your prototype's natural harmonics align with these lines, it is worth investigating further — especially if the alignment occurs without deliberate tuning.
Phi Ratio — The Golden Frequency
φ = 1.6180339887... and its role in resonant systems
▾

The Golden Ratio φ (phi) = 1.6180339887... is an irrational number with a unique mathematical property: it is the only number where φ² = φ + 1. It appears throughout nature in spiral growth patterns, planetary orbital ratios, crystal structures, and biological proportions.

In frequency space, a phi-ratio cascade means each frequency in the series is φ times the previous one. Unlike harmonic series (which are integer multiples), phi-ratio series are non-integer — this means they never produce destructive interference with each other, making them theoretically ideal for energy accumulation in resonant structures.

Base × φ⁻² = Base × 0.382
Base × φ⁻¹ = Base × 0.618
Base = Base × 1.000 ← fundamental
Base × φ¹ = Base × 1.618
Base × φ² = Base × 2.618
Base × φ³ = Base × 4.236
RESEARCH TIP
Enable the Phi-Ratio overlay in FFT tab and set your fundamental frequency as the base. Purple dashed lines appear at φ¹, φ², φ³ above and φ⁻¹, φ⁻² below. If your prototype spontaneously produces energy at these frequencies without being explicitly driven there — that is a significant finding worth documenting carefully.
CSV Data Format — Oscilloscope Export
How to export data from your oscilloscope and import it here
▾

CSV stands for Comma Separated Values. It is a plain text file where each line contains one measurement — the time in seconds and the voltage, separated by a comma. Every oscilloscope with a USB port or SD card can export this format.

time_s, voltage_V
0.0000000, 0.00000
0.0002500, 0.11769
0.0005000, 0.23465
0.0007500, 0.35017
0.0010000, 0.46353
... (continues for all samples)
Rigol Oscilloscopes
Insert USB drive → Storage menu → Type: CSV → Save. The file will contain header rows — the analyzer ignores non-numeric lines automatically.
Hantek / Owon
File → Export → Select CSV format. Some models export voltage only — if so, add a time column in Excel: first column = row number × (1 ÷ sample rate).
ACCEPTED FORMATS
The analyzer accepts commas, tabs, or semicolons as separators. Header rows are skipped automatically. Minimum 4 samples required. No maximum — the tool handles thousands of points efficiently.
